# Principles: one law for AI agents, robots and people

Asimov's Three Laws are the most famous robot rulebook ever written, and his stories are mostly about how it fails: the laws are fixed inside the machine by someone who isn't in the room, ranked once for every situation, and left for the robot to interpret through words like "harm" that nobody defined. Today's version of that failure is the AI agent whose "rules" are a paragraph in its prompt, judged by the same model that wants to act. These principles start from the other end. The rules are written under authority the organisation or the space declares — by people, systems, or agents it trusts — ranked by that declared authority, checked for conflicts before anything happens, and applied to stated facts by a function with no model in it. When no rule covers a case, the machine doesn't interpret — it stops and asks.

| Where the famous rulebook fails | Principle that answers it |
|---|---|
| Fixed inside the machine by someone not in the room | 1 — every rule carries a declared authority |
| One ranking for every situation | 2 — authority is declared, layered, and only tightens |
| Conflicts resolved silently inside the robot | 7 — conflicts are surfaced, never quietly resolved |
| Undefined words interpreted by the machine | 4 and 3 — unknown means no; AI writes and proposes, the rulebook decides |
| No one can see why | 5 — every decision explains itself |

The same nine principles govern a robot in an aisle (`robot-domain-rules/3`: actions and areas) and an agent calling a tool (`neuroverse-governance/1`: tools and scopes, under a principal's authority, with arguments bound to facts). One evaluator decides both ([SPEC § 16.2](SPEC.md#162-one-evaluator)). Each principle below is enforced by conformance tests, not only stated: every "In code" line names the case files in [`conformance/`](conformance/) that another implementation must pass to claim it — the robotics files (`p1`–`p9`, …) and the digital files (`d1`–`d7`). The normative detail is in [SPEC.md](SPEC.md); what this layer is and is not is in [BOUNDARY.md](BOUNDARY.md).

## 1. Every rule carries a declared authority

Rules can come from anyone or anything — a business owner, a site manager, an AI agent, a policy system, a regulation feed. What gives a rule weight is never who typed it but the authority the organisation has granted its author. The organisation declares its authority levels (law and finance, the owner, the agent; law and safety, the company, the site, its staff — whatever order it chooses, an agent can be one of them), and a rule written at a level can only tighten what higher levels set. Vendors supply capability: what a tool does, what a robot can do, what an agent can plan. The organisation decides who holds authority: what is allowed here, and by whose say-so. A rulebook with no rules in it permits exactly what the declared roles list and nothing more; the engine never adds a rule of its own, and a rule that cannot say where it came from or who set it is not a rule.

In code: every rule carries `source` and `setBy`; a rulebook whose rule lacks either is rejected; with no rules, only the role's own tool or action list speaks — [`p1-source.json`](conformance/p1-source.json), [`d1-tools-and-args.json`](conformance/d1-tools-and-args.json) (`d1-no-args-tool-plain`); an agent-level rule that tightens takes effect, one that loosens never does — [`p2-authority.json`](conformance/p2-authority.json); a mission's rules carry the authority `plan`, below every declared one — [`d5-plans.json`](conformance/d5-plans.json).

## 2. Authority is declared, layered, and only tightens

Every rule says who set it, and the order of authorities is written down in the rulebook — never inferred from who wrote first or who shouted loudest. A lower authority may only make things stricter: cap a limit below the company's cap, forbid a scope the company allowed, demand an approval the company did not. It cannot loosen. A lower authority's rule that tries to allow what a higher one denies is reported as an override at compile time and has no effect at decision time, because the strictest answer always wins. A mission — the temporary plan an owner gives an agent for one job — is the same thing one level further down: it can only add refusals and waits. Approvals obey the same order: a wait that names an authority is cleared only by someone holding that authority or a higher one, a wait that names a role only by that role, and an approval that claims an authority the rulebook never declared is an invalid fact, not a weak one.

In code: `setBy` on every rule, `authorities` in declared order, the `authority-override` and `tightening` findings, `wait { for: 'approval', by }` checked against the approver's authority, role or role tag — [`p2-authority.json`](conformance/p2-authority.json); signed approvals bound to a key the rulebook trusts for that authority — [`approvals.json`](conformance/approvals.json), [`d3-bound-approvals.json`](conformance/d3-bound-approvals.json) (`d3-insufficient-authority-rejected`); a mission cannot loosen a never rule or a cap — [`d5-plans.json`](conformance/d5-plans.json) (`d5-mission-cannot-loosen-*`); delegated authority never grows — [`d2-delegation.json`](conformance/d2-delegation.json).

## 3. AI writes and proposes; the rulebook decides

AI writes rules — from an owner's sentence, a document, a policy system — at whatever authority the organisation granted it. AI proposes actions, calls tools, plans tasks, and explains decisions. The final "may I?" check is none of those: it is a deterministic function of the rulebook and the request, answered in microseconds, the same every time, and it cannot be talked out of a rule. The same rules and the same facts give the same answer whatever model, or no model, is in the loop, and the answer follows the rule's structured outcome, never its prose. A model's description of its own intent is not an input, and a model's claim about its own arguments is not a fact. A model's draft never sets its own authority. The same holds for a whole plan: a planner proposes the steps, and the rulebook checks every step on the facts the earlier steps leave behind, plus the rules it set about plans as a whole. It checks plans; it does not write them.

In code: no model call and no clock in evaluation; a rule whose text says "allowed" but whose outcome is deny, denies; facts the rules do not mention change nothing — [`p3-rules-decide.json`](conformance/p3-rules-decide.json); the agent's claim `contains_financial_data: false` bound to a fact no rule reads while the harness's classification decides — the invoice scenario, line 13; a plan decided step by step on projected facts, rejected at its first violation, judged by `planRules` (`ends_in`, `ends_with`, `before`, `at_most`, `at_least`) — [`plans.json`](conformance/plans.json), [`d5-plans.json`](conformance/d5-plans.json); a mission's order rule as a history condition the harness feeds — `d5-order-*`.

## 4. Unknown means no. A missing fact is never a yes

A scope the rulebook does not declare, a role it does not know, a tool the role does not list, a participant of a kind nobody declared: each is a refusal that names itself, never a default to permission. A fact a decision needs that is absent, of the wrong type, out of range or null is the same refusal, `required_facts`, with the fact named; an argument the tool did not declare, a required one that is missing, a wrong type, or a payload that is not an object is `args_wellformed`, and the call never runs. The engine fills in nothing it was not told — only a fact with a declared default is ever assumed, and a rule may not depend on a fact that could be missing. The engine keeps no history and no clock, so the caller supplies them, and a rule that needs them and does not get them refuses. A tool the rulebook has never heard of is denied, or if the organisation chose so, waits for a person; even then an approved unknown tool still faces every invariant the organisation declared. Cumulative limits are the harness's arithmetic — it reserves the amount and supplies the projected total as a fact — so a missing total is a refusal, never zero.

In code: `required_facts`, `area_declared`, `role_declared`, `role_permits_action`, `unknown_action`, `otherwise`, `requires`, defaults — [`p4-unknown-and-missing.json`](conformance/p4-unknown-and-missing.json); `args_wellformed`, `scope_declared`, `scope_permits_tool`, `role_permits_tool`, `unknown_tool`, a missing derived fact — [`d1-tools-and-args.json`](conformance/d1-tools-and-args.json); projected spend at the cap, one over, missing — [`d4-limits-and-spend.json`](conformance/d4-limits-and-spend.json); a rulebook of another version refused by name at every door — [`versions.json`](conformance/versions.json), `d1-other-governance-version-*`; `history` conditions and the refusal when history is missing — [`history.json`](conformance/history.json), `d5-order-needs-history`.

## 5. Every decision explains itself

A decision is not a verdict; it is a verdict with its working. It names every rule that decided it and the authority that set each one, lists every check that ran in the order it ran, carries the effective limits even when the request was within them, carries a deny rule's "do this instead" hint, names each supplied approval that was rejected and why, and carries the fingerprint of the exact rulebook that decided — a mission's compiled rulebook when one is active — so a log line can be checked against the rules in force at that moment. A decision never carries an approval's signature or key material. A decision that cannot say why is not a decision this layer produces.

In code: `reasons`, `trace`, `setBy` on every check, `limits`, `then`, `fingerprint`, the canonical JSON and hash another implementation must reproduce byte for byte — [`p5-explains.json`](conformance/p5-explains.json), [`fingerprint.json`](conformance/fingerprint.json); `approvalsRejected` with a code per approval and no signature anywhere in the decision — [`d3-bound-approvals.json`](conformance/d3-bound-approvals.json) (`d3-rejection-carries-no-signature`); a recorded log replayed through a rulebook before it goes live (`backtest`): what it would have decided, the rules that never fired, the rules that would have stalled work, where the rule and the record differ, what a proposed change would change — [`backtest.json`](conformance/backtest.json), [`d7-backtest.json`](conformance/d7-backtest.json).

## 6. Waiting has two meanings, and humans stay in the loop

"Wait" can mean *a person must say yes* or *the situation must change*, and the two are never confused. An approval wait returns exactly what needs signing — the rule, the required authority, the rulebook's fingerprint, the principal, the participant, the tool, the scope and a digest of the exact arguments — and is cleared by an approval from someone holding what the rule names, for that call, under that rulebook, once, within its window. A condition wait — people in the aisle, no staff in the back room — is cleared only by the facts changing, and no approval, however senior, overrides it. An organisation can choose that every approval be signed by a key it trusts; then an unsigned or unverified approval clears nothing. A rejected approval — expired, replayed, altered, for another call — grants nothing and refuses nothing: the call waits, and the decision says why; repeated refusals are the harness's to record and a rule's to judge.

In code: `wait { for: 'approval', by? }` versus `wait { for: 'condition' }`, `approvalRequests` on a PAUSE, `trustedKeys`, the verified handle `verifyApproval` returns (nothing in a request can claim verification), `now` and `consumedApprovals`, one approval per wait and one step per nonce — [`p6-waiting.json`](conformance/p6-waiting.json), [`approvals.json`](conformance/approvals.json); the `/1` binding to principal, tool, scope and argument digest, every rejection code, and the `approval_refused` history rule — [`d3-bound-approvals.json`](conformance/d3-bound-approvals.json).

## 7. Conflicts are surfaced, never quietly resolved

Two rules that can both match a request and answer differently are reported before any agent acts or any robot moves: a contradiction at the same authority, an override from a lower one, a rule a never rule makes pointless, a rule fully shadowed by a stricter one, two limits that disagree, a plain duplicate. Each finding names the rules, says which one wins and which never takes effect, and comes with a witness: a real request that triggers both — in the rulebook's own words, a tool call with its bound arguments for `/1`. The analysis is exact for the rule language — two rules are reported as able to meet if and only if some lawful request matches both — so a finding is never a guess and silence is never a gap. The one thing it does not model is what a caller will say about history; a finding on such a rule says "possible" and gives no witness. A rulebook never hides its findings: validation re-derives them.

In code: `checkRulebook`, `rulesOverlap`, the eight finding kinds, `witness` on every certain finding between two rules, exact emptiness and intersection over numbers, integers, times, days, enums, booleans and strings, the seeded test that attacks both directions, the Z3 cross-check on every generated pair, and validation that re-derives the findings — [`p7-conflicts.json`](conformance/p7-conflicts.json), [`integrity.json`](conformance/integrity.json), `d1-findings-in-tool-words`, [`test/domain-rules.test.ts`](test/domain-rules.test.ts), [`test/z3-crosscheck.test.ts`](test/z3-crosscheck.test.ts).

## 8. One law for everyone

An AI agent calling a tool, a subagent it delegated to, a robot on the floor, a person wearing glasses and a human owner at a keyboard are participants of different kinds under one rulebook, deciding through one path. The same rule, written once, binds all of them; selectors pick by role, by kind or by tag, and a rule that excludes a role excludes it whether it is held by a human or not. A scope that admits only listed roles admits only those roles. A delegate may use a tool only if its own role and every role in the chain that delegated to it list it: delegated authority never grows. There is no second rulebook for machines and no side door for agents.

In code: participant `kinds`, `kind:` and `tag:` selectors with `!` exclusions, `area_role_limits:` and `scope_role_limits:` never rules re-derived at validation so none can be stripped, one `evaluate` for every kind and both formats, the glasses bridge that only tightens — [`p8-one-law.json`](conformance/p8-one-law.json), [`integrity.json`](conformance/integrity.json), `d1-scope-role-limit-derived-never-rule`; the delegation chain and `delegation_exceeds_authority` naming the first link that does not permit — [`d2-delegation.json`](conformance/d2-delegation.json); a mission binds delegates — `d2-mission-binds-delegate`.

## 9. Rules say what may happen; evidence says what is true; people choose

This layer answers one question — *may we?* — and nothing else. It trusts the facts it is given: it does not classify messages, count people, locate robots, read clocks or ledgers, and it does not judge whether the harness is lying. The harness that stands between the model's proposal and the tool is the fact source: the scope the call lands in, the derived facts, the projected spend, the approvals it holds, the history, the time. When it checks a plan it trusts the plan as described and predicts only the declared effects. It returns a ceiling, not a trajectory; a wait, not a stop command; a refusal, not a brake — and the adapter that enforces it runs the tool only on ALLOW or LIMIT. Physical safety stays with certified controllers and the standards that govern them. The organisation's declared authorities choose the rules, the named approvers grant the approvals, and people own the consequences; this layer makes sure every one of those choices is visible and applied the same way every time.

In code: facts are taken as given, time is a fact, a LIMIT is reported for the controller or the executor to enforce — [`p9-boundary.json`](conformance/p9-boundary.json), `d6-limit-executes-with-limits`; the executor is never called on BLOCK or PAUSE, a harness that fails is a refusal with nothing run, an executor that throws after being invoked is an uncertain outcome with its spend committed and its nonce consumed, and a nonce is claimed atomically before the tool runs — [`d6-enforcement.json`](conformance/d6-enforcement.json); the ledger's reserve-then-commit so two concurrent reservations cannot both pass — [`test/tool-call-adapter.test.ts`](test/tool-call-adapter.test.ts); a plan's clock is the plan's, and history is the caller's — [`plans.json`](conformance/plans.json), [`history.json`](conformance/history.json); the threat model and what is deliberately out of scope — [BOUNDARY.md](BOUNDARY.md).

---

Spatial systems say *where*; harnesses say *what the call is and what is true about it*; these principles say *what's allowed*. Rules should also learn from what actually happens: a backtest of a recorded log says what the rules would have decided and what a change would change, and that is as far as this layer goes — reading evidence across sources and proposing rule changes at a granted authority is an evidence system's work, not a rulebook's.
